How our SLOs are built

This page is for those who want to know more about Packit's Service Level Objectives (SLOs): the Service Level Indicators (SLIs) we use to track them, how we measure them and why we picked them. Other teams can use it as a guide when defining their own SLOs.

Job outcomes​

The objectives talk about jobs (builds and tests) ending with a status. To make them unambiguous, we distinguish these outcomes:

  • success: the job finished and everything is awesome
  • failure: the job finished and wasn't successful: the PR needs to be fixed
  • error: infrastructure problems prevented the job from completing

Only success and failure count as good outcomes for our objectives. Both give you a real answer about your change. An error means Packit (or a service it depends on) let you down.

Indicators​

To know how we are doing against the objectives, we measure these indicators:

ObjectiveIndicators
SLO1Time it takes Packit to set the first status on a PR or commit after receiving the event. Number of PRs or commits whose first status took longer than 25 seconds (a looser limit than the 15 seconds of SLO1).
SLO2Number of Copr builds queued, started and finished. Time from a Copr build finishing to Packit reporting the final status. Time from accepting a Copr build to it finishing.
SLO3Number of test runs queued, started and finished. Time from submitting a test run to Packit setting its final status.

The counters tell us how many jobs entered and left the pipeline. If jobs are queued or started but never finish, it shows up as a gap between the numbers. The durations tell us how long our users wait.

Both SLO1 indicators are recorded only when Packit sets a first status. An event that Packit drops completely, without setting any status, does not show up in either of them.

How we measure​

All the other teams around us use the Prometheus + Grafana combo, so this is our choice as well. Packit pushes the metrics to a Prometheus Pushgateway, and the indicators are visualised in two Grafana dashboards: Packit and Fedora CI.

Durations are recorded as Prometheus histograms. The histogram buckets are aligned with the SLO time thresholds (for example 15 seconds for SLO1 and 12 hours for SLO3), so we can directly compute what percentage of the measured jobs finished within the threshold, instead of relying on averages.

The histograms record only the time, whatever the outcome of the job. This means the current indicators approximate SLO2 and SLO3 rather than compute them exactly:

  • The time to the final test status is recorded for every finished test run, including runs that ended with an error or were canceled. A Testing Farm error reported within 12 hours therefore counts as within the threshold.
  • The time to the final build status is recorded only when Packit reports success or failure. A build that ends with an error on Packit's side is missing from the histogram completely.
  • Packit reports every unsuccessful Copr build as a failure, including builds that failed because of a Copr infrastructure problem. Such builds are measured as failures, even though they are errors by the definitions above.
  • A failed build is not reported, and so not measured, when its SRPM build has already failed (Packit has reported that failure already) or when a newer build for the same target exists.

Unfortunately, we cannot share our Grafana instance with you since it's hidden behind a firewall.

Why these objectives and indicators​

Before defining the objectives, we asked the most active users of Packit (projects such as anaconda, cockpit, systemd and others) what they expect from the service in terms of latency, reliability and availability. The objectives are derived from their answers.

SLO1: A quick first status​

The most consistent request was a quick "accepted" status: an indication that Packit has seen the change and will deal with it. Users were fine with waiting minutes or even hours for the results, as long as they knew the request had been picked up. Their expectations for this status ranged from 5 seconds to a minute. No status at all is frustrating, both for our users and for us, because it's impossible to tell whether Packit is working or has dropped the event. Hence the first objective is about the first status only, with a strict time limit and a high percentage.

In late 2022, the SLO1 limit was raised from 15 to 30 seconds to cover setting all statuses (packit-service#1776). In March 2023, SLO1 was changed to the current definition: the first status within 15 seconds.

SLO2: Builds end with a real answer​

Copr builds for pull requests are Packit's core functionality. What matters to users is that a build ends with a real answer (success or failure) and is not interrupted by an infrastructure error. Users told us an error rate of about 5 to 10% is something they would tolerate at the beginning.

Build duration is not something we control: some builds take minutes, others take hours. An objective defined from the moment we accept the build would therefore depend mostly on the project being built. That's why SLO2 measures the part Packit is responsible for: reporting the final result once Copr has finished the build.

SLO3: Tests with a lower target​

Tests are similar to builds, but they run in Testing Farm, which is outside of our control and is a more complex system (provisioning machines, running tests, collecting logs). That's why the percentage is lower and the time limit covers the whole test run.

Indicators follow the objectives​

We did not start by collecting all the metrics we could get. We picked indicators that answer the question "are we meeting the objective?" directly: how long it takes to set a status, and how many jobs entered and left each stage. This keeps the dashboards small and the alerts meaningful.

Guidance for other teams​

If you are defining SLOs for your own service, this is what worked for us:

  • Ask your users first. Talk to the most frequent users of your service and ask about latency, error rate, throughput and availability. Their answers are more valuable than the current numbers in your dashboards.
  • Start small. Some teams (for example CKI) have a comprehensive set of SLOs. Instead of taking the same route, we started with three objectives and add more objectives and indicators over time.
  • Measure what you control. If a part of the workflow is owned by another service (Copr builds, Testing Farm), either measure only your part or set a lower target.
  • Use distributions, not averages. "99% of requests within 15 seconds" says much more than "15 seconds on average", which can hide problems in the tail.
  • Avoid 100%. Insisting on perfection slows down development. The error budget gives you room to ship new features while keeping the service reliable.
  • Revisit the targets. Loose targets are fine at the beginning. Tighten them, or drop objectives nobody talks about, as you learn more.
